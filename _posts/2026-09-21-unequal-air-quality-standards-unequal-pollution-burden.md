National air quality standards are one of the clearest signals governments can send about the level
of pollution they consider acceptable. Yet the global regulatory landscape for fine particulate matter
(PM₂.₅) remains highly uneven. Across the 252 countries and territories covered by this analysis, only
96 have adopted a national annual PM₂.₅ standard (Figure 1). Those jurisdictions account for approximately
81.9 percent of the global population covered by the analysis, underscoring that the global challenge
is not only whether a standard exists, but how protective that standard is and whether countries can
actually attain it.

The 2021 World Health Organization (WHO) air quality guidelines provide a useful benchmark. WHO
recommends an annual PM₂.₅ concentration of {{ site.who_guideline }} µg/m³ and sets interim targets
of 35, 25, 15, and 10 µg/m³ to support stepwise progress toward that guideline (WHO, 2021). When
national standards are placed against this ladder, a striking pattern emerges: much of the world
technically lives under PM₂.₅ regulation, but a large share of that regulation remains substantially
less protective than WHO health-based benchmarks.

National annual PM2.5 standards vary widely across countries and territories. Many jurisdictions still
lack a standard, while many existing standards remain far above WHO guideline levels.

## 1. Most jurisdictions still lack a national PM2.5 standard

Of the 252 countries and territories covered by the analysis, 156 have no official national annual
PM2.5 standard. The absence of standards is geographically widespread, with the largest number of
jurisdictions without standards in Africa. Country counts alone, however, can be misleading: a small
island territory and a country with hundreds of millions of people each count as one jurisdiction.
Population-weighted measures therefore provide a more meaningful picture of regulatory coverage.

Approximately 81.9 percent of the global population covered by the analysis lives in a country or
territory with a national annual PM2.5 standard, while about 18.1 percent does not. The global
regulatory gap is therefore better described as two related problems: a large number of jurisdictions
still lack standards, and many of the standards that do exist remain weak relative to WHO guidance.

## 2. Air quality ambition differs sharply across countries

The WHO framework is explicitly designed as a progression: 35 µg/m³ (Interim Target 1), 25 (IT-2),
15 (IT-3), 10 (IT-4), and ultimately the {{ site.who_guideline }} µg/m³ guideline. This distinction
matters because simply classifying countries as “regulated” or “unregulated” hides enormous variation
in the level of protection implied by national standards.

Among people living in countries that have an annual PM₂.₅ standard, approximately 78.1 percent live
in jurisdictions where the standard is 15 µg/m³ or higher. That is roughly 64 percent of the global
population covered by the analysis (Figure 3). A standard of 35 µg/m³, for example, permits an annual
concentration seven times the WHO guideline; a standard of 40 µg/m³ permits eight times the
guideline.

The global landscape is therefore better understood as a continuum of regulatory ambition: no
standard, standards aligned with the least stringent WHO interim targets, progressively stronger
standards, and finally standards approaching the WHO guideline.

Although most jurisdictions lack a national annual PM₂.₅ standard, most of the global population
lives in countries that do have one. However, much of that population remains covered by standards
that are weak relative to WHO interim targets.

## 3. Weaker regulatory ambition coincides with higher pollution and larger longevity losses

The policy relevance of these differences becomes clearer when national standards are compared with
actual pollution exposure. AQLI combines satellite-derived annual PM₂.₅ estimates with population
data to characterize the concentrations experienced by people rather than averaging pollution
uniformly across land area.

According to AQLI’s 2024 satellite-derived, population-weighted PM₂.₅ estimates, countries with
national standards below 15 µg/m³ had an average population-weighted concentration of approximately
12.5 µg/m³. Across countries with standards of 15 µg/m³ or higher, together with countries lacking a
national standard, the corresponding concentration was approximately 29.0 µg/m³.

AQLI translates sustained particulate pollution exposure into its effect on life expectancy. Based
on the Huai River research underlying the Index, a sustained increase of 10 µg/m³ in PM₂.₅ is
associated with approximately {{ 10 | times: site.aqli_coefficient | round: 2 }} years of reduced life
expectancy (Chen et al., 2013; Ebenstein et al., 2017). Applying this relationship to 2024 exposure
shows a similarly large gap across regulatory groups: populations in countries with standards below
15 µg/m³ lose about 0.7 years of life expectancy relative to the WHO guideline, compared with
approximately 2.4 years among populations living under standards of 15 µg/m³ or higher or with no
national standard.

This difference should not be interpreted as the causal effect of adopting a stricter standard.
Regulatory stringency is correlated with income, industrial structure, energy systems, institutional
capacity, geography, historical pollution, and many other determinants of air quality. The result is
descriptive: countries with weaker or absent standards also tend to be those where pollution exposure
and AQLI-estimated longevity losses are substantially higher.

## 4. The next WHO target offers a practical policy benchmark

For countries with annual PM2.5 concentrations several times higher than the WHO guideline,
immediately reaching {{ site.who_guideline }} µg/m³ may be difficult. WHO’s interim targets are
useful precisely because they provide a sequence of increasingly protective milestones. A practical
AQLI counterfactual is therefore to ask how much life expectancy could be gained if countries reduced
sustained PM2.5 exposure to the next more stringent WHO step.

In this scenario, countries without a national PM₂.₅ standard would move toward 35 µg/m³; countries
with standards above 35 would move to 35; those between 25 and 35 would move to 25; those between 15
and 25 would move to 15; those between 10 and 15 would move to 10; and those between 5 and 10 would
move to the {{ site.who_guideline }} µg/m³ guideline. Countries already at or below 5 would remain
there.

If sustained PM2.5 concentrations were reduced to these next-step targets, population-weighted global
life expectancy would increase by approximately 0.74 years, equivalent to about 5.9 billion total
life-years across the global population represented in the analysis.

Crucially, this is a target-attainment scenario, not an estimate of the causal impact of passing a
law. A legal standard has no direct health effect if pollution remains unchanged. The life-expectancy
gains arise from actually reducing sustained PM2.5 exposure.

## 5. Standards establish ambition; implementation determines outcomes

National standards remain important because they establish measurable objectives against which air
quality can be monitored, policies designed, and progress evaluated. But standards must be paired
with the capacity to attain them. Depending on local sources, this can require controls on power
generation and industry, cleaner household energy, transport and fuel standards, agricultural
interventions, dust controls, and sufficiently robust monitoring to determine whether pollution is
actually falling.

The global air quality challenge is therefore no longer captured by the question, “Does a country
have a PM2.5 standard?” The more consequential questions are: How stringent is the standard? How
far is current exposure from that level? And are policies strong enough to close the gap?

The WHO interim targets provide a practical pathway. For highly polluted countries, progress need not
occur in a single leap from today’s concentrations to {{ site.who_guideline }} µg/m³. Moving
successively toward 35, 25, 15, 10, and ultimately {{ site.who_guideline }} µg/m³ creates measurable
milestones. Because sustained PM2.5 exposure has a quantifiable effect on longevity, each durable
reduction can translate into longer lives.

The next frontier in global air quality policy is thus not merely broader adoption of standards, but
progressively stronger standards backed by policies capable of delivering cleaner air.

## Methodological note

Developing a national standard takes effort in terms of current technological/ abatement and monitoring
resources in the country as well as policy and legal/constitutional framework of the country. The
dynamic nature of these conditions makes it hard to keep tabs on national standards as countries adopt
and update standards over time. Team at AQLI have developed a tracker for National Annual PM2.5
standards. It’s a dynamic tracker and we encourage readers to look at it and help us in keeping the
values updated. You can find the details
[here](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7454512654582398976/). This analysis
combines national annual PM₂.₅ standards with AQLI’s 2024 satellite-derived, population-weighted
annual PM₂.₅ estimates. It covers 252 countries and territories, of which 96 have a recorded national
annual PM₂.₅ standard. Life-expectancy impacts are interpreted using the relationship in which a
sustained 10 µg/m³ increase in PM₂.₅ corresponds to approximately
{{ 10 | times: site.aqli_coefficient | round: 2 }} years of reduced life expectancy. Comparisons
across regulatory categories are descriptive and should not be interpreted as causal estimates of
the effect of regulation.
